
# The Mechanical → CS Story

> **⭐⭐⭐ This is the single most important file in this folder for you.**
>
> You will be asked about the switch in **every interview** — technical rounds, HR rounds, and
> informally over coffee. It is the most visible unusual thing on your resume. Some version of
> *"you did Mechanical at IIITDM and now you're doing CSE at IIT Madras — what happened?"* is
> guaranteed.
>
> Get this right and it becomes an asset: evidence of self-direction, of the ability to learn a
> field from scratch, and of genuine motivation rather than default drift. Get it wrong — sound
> defensive, dismissive of your B.Tech, or vague about the motivation — and it plants a doubt that
> colours everything else.

---

## 1. What the interviewer is actually checking ⭐⭐⭐

They are **not** judging you for switching. Career changes are normal. They are checking four
things, in this order:

```
1. IS THE MOTIVATION REAL?     Did you move TOWARD something, or just AWAY from something?
                                "I didn't like mechanical" is a push story. Push stories worry
                                people, because the next thing you don't like might be this job.

2. ARE YOU COMMITTED?          Will you switch again in two years? Have you invested enough
                                that this is settled?

3. CAN YOU LEARN HARD THINGS?  You absorbed a CS degree's worth of fundamentals in a couple
                                of semesters. That is the impressive part — use it.

4. ARE YOU DEFENSIVE?          ⚠️ This is the real test. How you handle the question matters
                                more than the content of the answer. Calm and brief reads as
                                settled; long and justifying reads as unresolved.
```

⭐ **The meta-point:** a 40-second, relaxed, slightly self-aware answer closes the topic. A
three-minute justification keeps it open and invites more probing.

---

## 2. The answer — 40 seconds ⭐⭐⭐

Write your own version of this. It must sound like you, not like a script.

> "Honestly, the parts of mechanical engineering I enjoyed most were always the computational ones
> — simulation, numerical methods, anything where the interesting problem was in the algorithm
> rather than the hardware. By third year I'd worked out that I wanted to go further in that
> direction than a mechanical degree would take me, so I prepared for GATE alongside my final year
> and moved into CSE at IIT Madras.
>
> The first two semesters were genuinely hard — I was picking up four years of CS fundamentals
> while taking graduate courses in them. But it settled, and I'm now TA-ing the Advanced Data
> Structures and Algorithms course, which is a reasonable sign it worked out."

**Why this version works:**
```
✅ PULL, not push — "drawn toward computation", not "didn't like mechanical"
✅ Shows it was a DELIBERATE, planned decision (GATE, alongside final year) not an accident
✅ Acknowledges the difficulty honestly rather than pretending it was easy
✅ CLOSES with evidence that it worked (the TA position) — a fact, not a claim  ⭐⭐⭐
✅ 40 seconds. Short enough to signal the topic is settled.
```

---

## 3. What not to say ⚠️⭐⭐

| Don't say | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| "I didn't like mechanical engineering." | Pure push story. Invites "what if you don't like this?" |
| "There are no jobs in mechanical." | Sounds mercenary and slightly bitter. Also invites "so you're here for the package?" |
| "Everyone was doing CS." | Signals you follow rather than choose |
| "My B.Tech was a waste of time." | Never disparage your own background — it reads as poor judgement about your own decisions ⚠️ |
| "I've always been passionate about coding since childhood." | Generic, unverifiable, and contradicted by four years of mechanical engineering |
| A three-minute chronological account | Length signals the topic is unresolved for you |
| "It's a long story..." | Never open with this |

⚠️ **The worst version is apologising for it.** You did a GATE, got into IIT Madras CSE, and now
teach the algorithms course. That is a success story. Deliver it as one.

---

## 4. Turning it into a strength ⭐⭐⭐

If you have room, or if the interviewer follows up with interest, these three framings are genuinely
persuasive:

### (a) You have proven you can learn a field cold
> "The thing the switch actually demonstrates is that I can pick up a field from nothing and get to
> a level where I can teach it. That's the skill I'd bring to a codebase or a domain I haven't seen
> before — I've already done the hard version of it once."

⭐ This is the strongest framing available to you, because it answers the question every employer
actually has: *can this person handle something unfamiliar?*

### (b) Mechanical engineering left you something real
> "Mechanical gave me a physical intuition for systems and constraints that I still use. When I'm
> thinking about memory bandwidth or a bottleneck in a pipeline, it's the same reasoning as a
> thermal or structural constraint — find the limiting resource and work on that. It also gave me
> the numerical-methods background that made the linear algebra in deep learning feel familiar
> rather than new."

⚠️ Keep this short and do not oversell it. One or two sentences. Stretching for a connection sounds
like rationalising.

### (c) You chose this deliberately, twice
> "I didn't drift into CS — I sat an entrance exam to get here and then chose a systems-heavy
> specialisation within it. Both were active choices."

## 5. The follow-up questions ⭐⭐

<details><summary>"Why didn't you do a CS B.Tech in the first place?"</summary>

> "Seventeen-year-old me picked a branch on the basis of a rank and not much information, like most
> people do. I got a much better idea of what I actually enjoyed by doing the degree, and I acted
> on it as soon as I was sure."

Calm, non-defensive, slightly self-aware. Do not blame the counselling system, your family, or your
rank at length — one clause is enough.
</details>

<details><summary>"How did you catch up on four years of CS?"</summary>

⭐ This is a **gift** — it invites you to describe exactly the self-direction they are screening for.
Be specific:
> "Mostly by front-loading the fundamentals. The first semester I took the core graduate courses
> and back-filled the undergraduate material underneath them in parallel — data structures,
> operating systems, the theory. The DSA one I went deepest on, partly because everything else
> depends on it, and that's ended up being what I TA. It was a hard two semesters and I won't
> pretend the CGPA didn't take the hit, but the fundamentals are solid now."

Naming the CGPA cost yourself, in passing, is disarming — it pre-empts the next question (§6) and
it makes you sound honest rather than managed.
</details>

<details><summary>"Do you regret the four years?"</summary>

> "No. I'd have got here sooner without them, but I'd also know less about how physical systems
> behave, and I wouldn't have found out what I wanted by reasoning about it in the abstract — I had
> to do the degree to know."
</details>

<details><summary>"Would you switch again?"</summary>

> "I don't think so. The difference is that I chose this one with information. The first time I was
> picking from a brochure; this time I'd done four years of engineering and knew what I actually
> wanted to spend my time on."

⭐ This directly answers the real concern (flight risk) without being defensive about it.
</details>

<details><summary>"You've only been doing CS for two years. Isn't that a disadvantage?"</summary>

> "For breadth, possibly — there are corners of CS I haven't touched that a four-year student
> would have. For the things I've gone into, I don't think so: I built the Transformer from
> scratch, I've written CUDA kernels and profiled them, and I TA the advanced algorithms course.
> And the two years were concentrated — I wasn't spreading them across a broad undergraduate
> curriculum."

Concede the real part of the point, then redirect to evidence. Never deny a fair point outright.
</details>

<details><summary>"Why an M.Tech rather than working after your B.Tech?"</summary>

> "Because I wanted to change fields, and doing that properly needed a degree behind it. Taking an
> entry-level mechanical job and trying to move sideways into software later would have been much
> slower and much less credible."
</details>

## 6. The linked question: the CGPA ⚠️

The switch story and the 7.5 CGPA are connected, and interviewers often ask them together. Full
treatment in `04-Difficult_Questions.md` §2, but the one-line version:

> "The first two semesters I was taking graduate CS courses while back-filling the undergraduate
> material underneath them. That cost me some marks, and I'd make the same trade again — the
> fundamentals mattered more than the grade."

⭐ Pre-empting it inside the switch answer (as in §5) is usually better than waiting to be asked
separately.

---

## 7. Calibrating by audience ⭐

```
TECHNICAL INTERVIEWER   : 20 seconds, then move on. They mostly want the fact, not the story.
                          "Mechanical B.Tech, moved to CSE through GATE, now TA the DSA course."

HR INTERVIEWER          : the full 40-second version (§2). They are assessing motivation
                          and commitment, which is what the long version addresses.

HIRING MANAGER          : the 40-second version PLUS framing (a) from §4 — the "I can learn a
                          field cold" angle is what they care about. ⭐

CASUAL / COFFEE CHAT    : the honest, relaxed version. This is where being at ease about it
                          matters most, because it is the least scripted setting.
```

---

## 8. Rehearsal ⭐⭐⭐

```
□ Write YOUR version. Not mine. It must contain a true, specific detail — the actual course
  or project in mechanical that pulled you, if there was one.
□ Say it aloud, timed. Target 40 seconds. Most people run to 2 minutes first time.
□ Record it once. Watch for: defensiveness in your tone, apologising, over-explaining,
  the word "basically", and trailing off at the end.  ⭐ Trailing off is the common tell.
□ Practise the 20-second version separately.
□ Practise the follow-ups in §5 aloud — especially "would you switch again?"
□ Then STOP rehearsing. Over-rehearsed sounds defensive, which is the exact failure mode.
```

**The test:** can you say it with a slight smile, in a relaxed tone, and then stop talking? If the
answer ends with you still talking, it is not ready.
